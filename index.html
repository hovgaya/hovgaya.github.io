The onomastic string

A name is an ordered sequence of segments ⟨s₁ … sₙ⟩, each recorded with form, type, script and source. Contact acts on segments: every change is one operation performed by one agent. Continuity is the share of core segments (ECC, RANK/TITLE, BASE, LIN, HEAD) that survive, measured against the naming template of the source environment.

How Armenian names change in the diaspora

This tab presents separate diaspora datasets (D-series). They are not part of the article's supplementary package and are not merged with the epigraphic data of the Map, Model and Corpus tabs.

The question is not whether Armenian names change outside the homeland but which operations change them, under which pressure and in what proportions. Two kinds of evidence answer it. Linked records pair each recorded name with a standard form, so the operation is visible directly. Unlinked lists give no pairs; there the Armenian surnames are first detected, and the profile of their spellings and of the given names borne with them is counted. Operations are coded on the segments of the name (prefix, stem, ending), following the segment types of the model.

At the port: the Armenian Immigration Project (North America, to 1939)

Each of entries from ship manifests and border crossings is compared with its standardised surname; one entry can show several operations. Most change falls on the stem and comes from spelling and transmission, not from the bearer's choice. Translation and truncation are rare at entry.

In present-day and twentieth-century lists: surname endings

Share of persons with an Armenian surname (detector tiers A–B) by ending. In Glendale the Eastern -yan rises from 44% to 49% of the city's Armenian-surnamed employees in thirteen years: post-Soviet immigration is overtaking the older Western diaspora in the public record. Spelling features

Given names borne with an Armenian surname

In Marseille the generational step is plain. Armenian national names fall from 47% among those born abroad to 9% among those born in France; Western equivalents of Armenian Christian names (Jean for Hovhannes, Jacques for Hagop, Pierre for Bedros) rise from 12% to 36%. The surname keeps its Armenian ending while the given name is translated or replaced: a split adaptation.

India, 1780–1861: the community register and the British court

Two collections of the FIBIS database record the same Calcutta community through two hands. The baptism register of the Holy Nazareth Church (1,259 entries, 1793–1859) was kept by the community and is transcribed from Armenian; the inventories of estates (730 entries, 1780–1861) were written by the British court. Every name was linked, where possible, to its Armenian base name and the operation classified. The lineage suffix is the clearest contrast: 121 of the 1,259 register entries carry -ean/-ian, none of the 730 inventory entries does. The court renders the father's or ancestor's given name as the surname, in English spelling (Arratoon for Harutyun, Carrapiet for Karapet), or replaces it with a European equivalent (Gregory, George, Peter).

A typology of modification

  1. Transmission spellings (stem). The same name written through the orthography of the receiving or transmitting language: French dj, tch, ch, ou, ss; English oo, gh; Russian loss of initial H (Oganesyan, Akopyan); Iranian-Armenian -eh in given names (Armineh, Atineh); Anglo-Indian court spellings ie, oo, silent -e, c for k, w for v (Carrapiet, Arratoon, Petruse, Kawork).
  2. Dialect consonantism (stem). Western b/g/d against Eastern p/k/t: Bedrosian/Petrosyan, Garabedian/Karapetyan.
  3. Ending replacement (ending). -ian ~ -yan ~ -jan ~ -ean; Russified -ov/-off; -iants/-yants; Turkish -oğlu (-oglou, -oghlian); Iranian-Armenian plural -ians. The lineage function survives the change of form.
  4. Prefix handling (prefix). Ter-/Der- and Hadji- kept apart, fused (Derderian, Deradourian) or dropped.
  5. Truncation (ending removed). Garabedian → Garabed, Sarkisian → Sarkis: the given name of the ancestor becomes the surname.
  6. Translation or replacement (whole name). Hagopian → Jacobson, Jackson; Bedrosian → Peters. No formal trace remains; only linked records reveal these.
  7. Given-name substitution. Calque equivalents (Hovhannes → Jean/John/Ivan, Kevork → Georges/George, Krikor → Grégoire), conventional and folk-etymological equivalents (Garabed → Charles, Harutyun → Arthur; in Calcutta Hovhannes → Owen, Melkum → Malcolm, Babajan → Babajohn), full replacement, and local spellings of Armenian names (Zarouhie, Siranouche).

How Armenian surnames are found in large lists

The detector (dataset D7) assigns a surname form a graded probability of Armenian origin, with its evidence: the ending, the stem (an Armenian given name or name-forming lexeme in Eastern or Western form), attestation in the immigration records or Wikidata, and penalties for known homographs (Brian, Ryan, Julian, Chinese Yan). Stems shared by Armenian and Iranian naming are flagged, not removed. Tested on the 24,839 surname forms of the immigration records, without using them as evidence, it placed 93.5% of entries in tiers A–C. It cannot see translated names. It scores forms, not persons.

Data: D-series datasets (Data tab). The detector's reference lexicon also draws on Wikidata (CC0). Names are treated as linguistic forms; a surname of Armenian origin says nothing settled about the identity of any person who bears it.

Wikipedia and Wikidata: the name before and after the move (D20)

Wikidata records many people of Armenian origin with their name in two scripts: the Armenian form and the form used in the country where they lived and worked. Each such record is a ready-made pair, and the pair shows which segment of the name changed. D20 gathers 5,581 persons: those recorded in Wikidata as ethnic Armenians, and those placed in English Wikipedia's categories of people of Armenian descent and Armenian emigrants. Of these, 2,375 were born outside the homeland or died abroad, and 3,937 carry a surname in both scripts.

The result is the split adaptation seen in the lists above, now traced to the place of birth. The surname base survives almost everywhere. The lineage suffix sorts by environment: -yan among those born in the homeland, -ian among those born in the Middle East, France and North America, and -ov/-ev among those born in Russia and the former Soviet Union, where it is usually already present in the Armenian form of the name.

    Use and ethics

    Names on this site are treated as linguistic forms. A surname of Armenian origin says nothing settled about the identity of any person who bears it. All tables are aggregates; no list of persons is published, example names are shown only for groups of 50 or more and names borne at least three times, and records whose publication was opposed (INSEE register) are excluded. Data and scripts are provided for onomastic and historical research and must not be used to profile or target individuals or groups.
